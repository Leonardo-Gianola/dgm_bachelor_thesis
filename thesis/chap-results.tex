\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

In this chapter we compare the four scheduler runs on
\texttt{swe\_verified\_mini} under the evaluation protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. We determine which scheduler is the most
cost-efficient, that is, which reaches a decent accuracy for the least
budget. All runs are single-seed (n=1) and the comparison is descriptive
only.

% =====================================================================
\section{Metric Validity}
\label{sec:results-validity}

Before showing any result, we establish which metrics are reported and
why an early-rung score is not a fair signal. The headline figure is
\texttt{accuracy\_score = resolved\_instances / submitted\_instances},
computed by SWE-bench in
\texttt{run\_evaluation.py:444} as \texttt{len(predictions)}. Every
prediction the agent emits is in the denominator, including empty
patches: an empty patch counts as one submitted instance with zero
resolved, so empties penalise the rate rather than inflating it.

The real validity issue is sample size at low rungs. A rung~0
evaluation runs the child on as few as 1 to 3 tasks, and DGM's native
per-generation selector picks \texttt{best\_child\_score} from
\texttt{dgm\_metadata.jsonl}. A child that resolves 1 of 1 tasks
therefore scores 100\%, which looks optimal but is a sampling
artefact. The evidence is how often Hyperband and ASHA register raw
100\% rates at rung~0.

To report unaltered, comparable results we use the final-rung accuracy
of the best promoted child of each generation. The initial archive
evaluated all four schedulers on the same fixed
\texttt{swe\_verified\_mini} subset and resolved 16 of 47 submitted
predictions (34.04\%), of which 11 predictions were empty patches.
Workload is reported with two complementary metrics in
Section~\ref{sec:results-compile}: \emph{intended} workload, summed
from each child's final-rung \texttt{cumulative\_budget\_size} (the
scheduler's plan), and \emph{actual exposure}, the count of
predictions SWE-bench produced (resolved + unresolved + empty). The
two diverge when children are killed mid-rung or when SWE-bench
retries instances inside a stage. All numbers in this chapter are
single-seed (n=1), with no repetition, no confidence interval, and no
significance test. Multi-seed replication was precluded by the
project's fixed API budget, which the four pilot runs exhausted. One
further validity caveat: GA's generation~1 was silently skipped by a
deterministic seed=0 selection bug, so GA has only two productive
generations. These threats are revisited in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}.

\section{Cost-Efficiency}
\label{sec:results-costscore}

Per-generation cost is not tracked; only run-total cost exists.
``Cost to reach threshold X'' therefore means the total cost of the
run whose peak clears X, not the cost paid up to the generation that
first crossed X. Concretely, if a run cleared X at generation~2 but
continued through generation~3, the figure still charges the whole
run, including the post-crossing generations that did not contribute
to clearing X. The reported number is an upper bound on the
early-stopping cost to threshold.

This section answers the central research question: which scheduler buys
the most accuracy per dollar. The Genetic Algorithm is the most efficient,
peaking at 70.59\% with a cost of \$0.71 per point, the sweet spot of
lowest run cost and best efficiency, and 34\% cheaper per accuracy point
than Hyperband. Up to the 70\% mark GA is the cheapest scheduler at every
threshold (Table~\ref{tab:results-threshold},
Figure~\ref{fig:cost-threshold}), and ASHA is strictly dominated by GA
there, reaching equal or lower accuracy at higher cost (\$39 versus
\$26). The cost-versus-peak frontier is plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:cost-vs-peak}. The Hyperband run that broke 75\% did so
with a budget 73\% larger than GA (\$44.98 for 5~pp more). Under a fixed
budget GA dominates unless a score above roughly 70\% is required, and in
that territory Hyperband is the only option, but at a steep premium.

\section{Per-Generation Trajectories}
\label{sec:results-accuracy}

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy-per-gen} and Table~\ref{tab:results-pergen}
show the per-generation best promoted child at the final rung, where
the rate is resolved/submitted with empties counted in the
denominator. The baseline rises slowly and monotonically,
plateauing across generations~1 and~2 (both 54.17\%) before a
generation-3 breakthrough, a gain of 25.5~pp over the initial archive. The
Genetic Algorithm lost its first two generations to different causes.
Generation~0 produced no archive-worthy child (compile failures and
rung-0 kills), and generation~1 was skipped entirely by the seed=0
selection bug. It then recovered to about 69\% and 70\%, the steepest
useful slope over only two generations. Hyperband rose consistently from
generation~0 to generation~1, dipped slightly at generation~2, and peaked
at about 75\%, a net upward but noisy trajectory. ASHA produced the best
generation-0 result, collapsed at generation~1, partially rebounded at
generation~2, and ended lowest at generation~3 (48.89\%). It is the only
scheduler finishing below its own generation~0. Rung schedulers (Hyperband
and ASHA) surface a strong child earlier than the baseline, but only
Hyperband sustained that trajectory. Per-generation child counts,

\section{Compile and Promotion Rates}
\label{sec:results-compile}

This section covers two gates: compile, then rung promotion. For
reliability the baseline is unmatched at 100\% compile, followed by ASHA
(75\%) and Hyperband (70\%), and the most erratic is the Genetic Algorithm
at 60\% (Figure~\ref{fig:compile-rate}). The argparse guard catches
broken children before evaluation, reducing budget waste. In evaluation
tasks spent (Table~\ref{tab:results-workload},
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-tasks}), ASHA
spends the most under both readings (346~planned, 368~actual).
Hyperband planned 324 tasks but child kills brought its actual
exposure to 250, close to the baseline's intended 253. GA is consistently the lightest at
roughly 100~tasks under either reading. For promotion, under 8\% of
Hyperband and
ASHA children reached the final rung, against 83\% for the baseline. This
is a design tradeoff: rung schedulers spend evaluation budget broad and
shallow and kill fast, versus the narrow and deep baseline. In general,
compile rate is inversely related to mutation aggression, and the rung
sweep cost produces many discarded children.

The intended/actual gap in Table~\ref{tab:results-workload} has two
mechanisms pulling in opposite directions. The first inflates
\emph{actual} above \emph{intended}: SWE-bench writes one prediction
JSON per evaluated stage, and a child promoted through multiple stages
is re-evaluated on the cumulative task set each time.
\texttt{get\_all\_performance} sums across all stage files, so an
instance that appears in three stages of a baseline child is counted
three times. A single baseline child progressing through
small~$\to$~medium~$\to$~full contributes
$3 + 15 + 50 = 68$ predictions to \emph{actual} while \emph{intended}
counts only the final-rung 50. This is why Baseline's actual~(324)
exceeds intended~(253), and ASHA shows a smaller version of the same
effect on promoted children (+22).

The second mechanism deflates \emph{actual} below \emph{intended}:
tasks where the agent produces no output at all (context-overflow
crashes on minimax-m2.5, docker timeouts) yield no prediction file and
drop from \texttt{submitted\_instances} entirely. Hyperband's wide
rung-0 sweep over 54 children amplifies this: 15 children produced
fewer predictions than their final-rung plan, against only 3 with
stage overlap, leaving a net 74-task deficit (intended~324,
actual~250). GA shows the same pattern in miniature ($-12$) on its
low-compile-rate population.

Table~\ref{tab:results-coverage} reports the best-of-run number
under both metrics side by side. The DGM-reported
\texttt{accuracy\_score} ranks Hyperband first (75.86\%) and Baseline
last (59.57\%), while \texttt{coverage\_rate} flips the order:
Baseline first (56.00\%), Hyperband last (44.00\%). The two
disagree because Hyperband's best child only emitted 29 of 50
prediction files (21 silent drops), whereas Baseline's best emitted
47 of 50, so Baseline pays the no-file penalty far less.

Each gap below 50 in the \emph{Submitted} column of
Table~\ref{tab:results-coverage} is a task for which the agent emitted
no \texttt{prediction.json} file at all. Three causes contribute, in
roughly decreasing order of frequency: minimax-m2.5 context-overflow
crashes on the harder repositories (Django, sympy), where the model
exceeds the input-token limit mid-solve and the agent loop terminates
without writing a patch; docker or harness exceptions inside
\texttt{harness.py} that abort the container before
\texttt{out\bk\_fname.write\bk\_text(...)} runs; and aggressive
mutations that break the agent itself, so it crashes on tasks where a
working agent would have at least produced an empty patch. Baseline
sits at $47/50$ because its error-log-guided mutations only edit
narrowly around past failures, leaving the agent runnable. Hyperband
and ASHA spawn fifteen children per generation with wider mutations,
so more children damage the agent and more tasks silently drop.

The headline accuracy in this chapter remains resolved/submitted,
that is, resolved per task the agent actually attempted. Under that
frame the four runs are compared on equal terms within each child's
realised attempts, and the planning-versus-realisation gap is a
separate measurement of \emph{coverage reliability}, reported in
Table~\ref{tab:results-coverage} alongside the accuracy but not
folded into it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

Under a fixed budget on this benchmark, the Genetic Algorithm achieves the
best accuracy per dollar (\$0.71 per point), with a competitive peak near
70\% at the lowest cost (\$26), despite compiling only 60\% and losing its
first two generations. The quality and budget tradeoff is clear: reaching
the top accuracy requires Hyperband, but that gain of about 5~pp comes at
a large cost increase (\$44.98, 73\% over GA). ASHA runs 32\% faster in
wall time than Hyperband (the async payoff) but at a lower peak and a
non-monotonic, regressing trajectory, two notable downsides of the async
tradeoff. The baseline is the only fully reliable scheduler (100\%
compile) but the lowest-peaking and most predictable. Every claim above is
bounded by n=1, no significance is possible at a single seed, and a
multi-seed re-run is left to future work.

\paragraph{On the choice of primary metric.}
This thesis keeps \texttt{accuracy\_score} as the headline because it
is the DGM-reported figure and is directly comparable to prior DGM and
SWE-bench Verified results obtained with stronger base models such as
Claude and GPT. Switching to \texttt{coverage\_rate} as the primary
ranking would break that line of comparison. The cost is that, under
minimax-m2.5, \texttt{accuracy\_score} silently rewards schedulers
whose children crash often: each silent no-file drop shrinks the
denominator instead of counting as a failure, and the more aggressive
the mutation, the larger the bias. Hyperband's headline lead
(75.86\%) is partly a denominator artefact of this kind, since its
best child emitted only 29 of 50 prediction files.
\texttt{coverage\_rate} reports the same four runs without that bias
(Table~\ref{tab:results-coverage}) and reverses the ranking, putting
Baseline first (56.00\%) and Hyperband last (44.00\%). On
minimax-m2.5, this is the more honest reading of the
scheduler-versus-scheduler comparison made in this thesis; on a
stronger base model with negligible crash rate the two metrics would
converge and the bias would disappear.
